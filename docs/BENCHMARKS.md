## Data parallelism against Rust and Go (ms, min / median)

Five of the kernels above, each ported to Rust and to Go with the same work, the same sizes and the same checksum: every binary below printed the Nish `par` run's output (numeric tokens to 1e-9 relative; the n-body energies agree to the last bit). The Rust twins run on `std::thread::scope` (`bench/par-<kernel>.rs`) and on Rayon's pool (`bench/par-<kernel>-rayon.rs`), the Go twins on goroutines and a `sync.WaitGroup` (`bench/par-<kernel>.go`). The map twins cut the array into one contiguous chunk per thread, the first on the calling thread, as Nish's partitioner did until it began cutting up to eight pieces per thread that the threads claim as they finish ([Scheduling a divided region](#scheduling-a-divided-region)); Rayon splits adaptively. par-histogram takes the lock once per value in every language, Nish's `Mutex`, Rust's `std::sync::Mutex` and Go's `sync.Mutex`, kept deliberately as a comparison of a contended lock. par-histogram-atomic bins the same values into an `AtomicI32` per bin, one sequentially consistent add per value in every language, Nish's `add`, Rust's `AtomicI32::fetch_add` with `SeqCst` and Go's `atomic.Int32.Add`: the same contention on sixteen bins without the lock. The version that avoids the contention, a local tally and one `add` per bin, is `examples/histogram-atomic.ts` ([the threads guide's atomics section](guide/using-nish/threads.md#counting-without-a-lock-atomici32-and-atomici64)).

**The same work, not the same checks or the same build.** Nish builds with its default checks: every integer operation that can overflow and every index it cannot prove in range is checked (`nish_panic_overflow` and `nish_panic_index` are reached from par-histogram's `binStream`, for one). The Rust twins build as `--release` does, with bounds checks and without overflow checks; Go checks bounds and not overflow. And Nish's `--profile speed` links with LTO, where neither Rust build does, which gives Nish a small edge. In par-nbody each Nish probe reaches the bodies through class references, where the twins index a contiguous array of structs, which slightly favours Rust and Go.

**How it is timed.** Every binary divides over 4 threads, the count Nish's runtime chooses on this machine: `NISH_NUM_THREADS`, `RAYON_NUM_THREADS`, `GOMAXPROCS` and `PAR_THREADS` (read by the scope twins) are all set to it. The comparison is run 3 time(s), minutes apart, each run 11 interleaved rounds after 1 warm-up: in each round every binary and a byte-identical copy of it run once, in an order rotated by one per round. The times below are process wall time, min / median in ms over every round of every run. In each run, and separately for the minimum and the median, Nish is divided by the fastest other column on that statistic, and the band is the larger of the gaps between Nish and its copy and between that column and its copy. A ratio reads as faster or slower only outside the band, and within 0.5% of its edge as at the edge. The reading takes every run together: runs that fall on both sides of the band make no claim. The Nish column is the `par` run of the table above, timed again here beside seven other binaries rather than in that table's alternation with its loop, so the two tables' Nish figures differ by about as much as this machine moves between runs: compare within a table, not across.

## Scheduling a divided region

Measured on 2026-10-20 on the machine of the data-parallelism table above (Intel(R) Xeon(R) Processor @ 2.10GHz, 4 logical cores), every figure a median in ms over interleaved rounds in which each binary and a byte-identical copy of it ran once; the copy's gap is the noise band, and a difference inside it is no difference.

`nish_parallel_range` used to cut a divided region into one contiguous chunk per thread. A thread that was preempted held every other thread at the join. It now cuts the range into up to eight pieces per thread, never smaller than the grain, and each thread claims the next piece with one relaxed `fetch_add` as it finishes the last; the calling thread runs the first. Eight is the flat middle of a sweep over par-heavy at four threads, nine rounds: 2 pieces per thread was slower and noisier (868, 766–1121), 4 to 32 lay within the copy band (805–822), and so did par-scale's memory-bound map (262–270).

| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| par-heavy, 4 threads, 15 rounds | 16 scramble rounds per element, 2^22 elements, 40 calls | 846 | 834 | 792 | 771 |
| par-scale, 4 threads | `x * k`, 2^22 elements, 40 calls: memory-bound | 277 | 266 | 270 | 260 |
| par-single, 4 threads | one `x * 3` map over 2^25 elements | 213 | 209 | 213 | 207 |
| par-alloc, 4 threads, 9 rounds | a string formatted per element, 2^22 elements: the body allocates | 88 | 79 | 52 | 51 |
| 3 chunks above the grain | par-heavy at 600,000 elements, 300 calls: three chunks of the grain | 1053 | 1036 | 1071 | 1174 |

par-heavy at one thread is 2172 (2108–2315), within main's band. Over that one-thread loop, on the medians of the same rounds, par-heavy goes from about 2.6x on `main` (2172 / 846) to about 2.7x (2172 / 792), about 6% faster at four threads and outside the copy band (846 / 834 against 792 / 771); the data-parallelism table above, on minima, has 2.91x. par-alloc, whose body allocates, is the largest gain: 79–88 ms on `main`, 51–52 here, and the gain is the pieces' own: the same runtime with the arena reset between pieces taken out measures 53, the same. par-histogram is a scope of tasks, not a divided region, and its contended lock spreads each binary over 89–258 ms; its medians, 223–229 on `main` and 199–206 here over 15 rounds, are no difference. The others are unchanged.

A single 2^25 map is not slower than at efbbd8c8 (212 / 211) or on `main`, and workers touching their own pages first (first-touch) measured within the band, so it is not shipped.

**par-heavy does not reach 3x, and the scheduler is not why.** Its checksum is an order-sensitive fold over the result, which runs on one thread, and so do the fill and the allocation before the map. Timed with zero scramble rounds, so that the map does nearly nothing, the program takes 252 with the fold and 75 without it: the fold is about 177 ms and the fill, the allocation and the near-empty map about 75, some 286 ms of the 2172 one-thread run (13%).

By Amdahl the best four cores can do is (2172 − 286) / 4 + 286 ≈ 757 ms, a cap of about 2.9x; the program measures about 2.7x on these medians. Inside the region each thread was busy for 500–544 ms of an ideal 471 (87–93% busy efficiency), the region's wall time was 555–591 ms (80–85%), threads started within 1.6–5 ms of each other and finished within 30–60 ms, and each claimed 152–171 pieces.

The fold, not the scheduler, explains `main`'s 2.6x. With the serial part made smaller or the divided part larger, the same map clears 3x on both runtimes: par-heavy-fold, whose fold vectorises, measures about 3.15x, and par-heavy4, with four times the scramble rounds and the same fold, about 3.4x.

## JSON against other languages

Written by `node bench/json-compare/run.mjs --record`, not by `bench/run.mjs`, which carries this section over when it rewrites the file. The run is its own, so the host below may not be the one in the table at the head of this file. The workloads, the checksums and the measurement rules are in [that script's header](https://github.com/amritk/nish/blob/main/bench/json-compare/run.mjs), and the Nish program is [main.ts](https://github.com/amritk/nish/blob/main/bench/json-compare/main.ts).

The ratio column divides the Nish minimum by that implementation's minimum, as the tables above do: above 1.00x it is faster than Nish. A ratio within the noise band, the gap between Nish and a byte-identical copy of its binary, is marked as no difference.

Builds: Nish `--link --profile speed`; C `clang -O3 -DNDEBUG` and C++ `clang++ -O3 -DNDEBUG`; serde_json cargo `--release` with LTO and one codegen unit; Go `go build`.

What differs between the rows:

- **Tokenize** is each library's own way of visiting every value. Nish's tokenizer copies every key and string out with `stringInto`; yyjson builds a whole tree, reading a copy of the input rather than in place; RapidJSON's Reader (SAX) calls back per value; serde_json drives a Visitor; Go's `Decoder.Token` makes an `interface{}` per token. simdjson On-Demand materialises every value as it is visited. What must agree: in the timed loop, each token's kind and each key's and string's length and last byte, folded, and the sum of the numbers as a double; in an untimed pass over the last iteration's input, every byte of every key and string.
- **Typed decode** has no yyjson or RapidJSON row: neither has a typed layer. Every row decodes into a fresh value each iteration. simdjson On-Demand reads `performances` and nothing after it, while serde_json, Go and Nish scan the whole document, so simdjson's MB/s divides the whole document's bytes by a time that did not read all of them. The extra Nish row decodes into one value reused across iterations, which nish/json-typed supports and the others' APIs do not. What must agree: every decoded field of every performance, each string byte by byte, folded each iteration.
- **Serialize** times the writing alone, from the same decoded values. Nish (`jsonEncode`) and serde_json (derive) write from the type, Go by reflection (its encoder's trailing newline is trimmed), and simdjson's row is builder calls written by hand, field by field, since simdjson has no type-driven writer without C++26 reflection. What must agree is a 64-bit `h * 31 + v` fold of every byte of every iteration's output, and the last output's length.

### `jsonEncode`'s keys, one pre-escaped copy

`jsonEncode` of a class writes each key with `JsonWriter.keyQuoted`: the compiler quotes and escapes the key once, at compile time, into a string constant (`"name":`), and the writer copies it in one go after the comma and indent. - **`plaintext`.**
  - The explicit entry answers inside `Http1Server`'s event loop and sends the 16 pipelined answers of one read in one `sendto`: 25,379 for 402,880 requests.
  - `serve` takes a connection's next request only once the one before it is answered (`std/net/serve.ts`, "at most one request per connection is in flight"), and `HttpHandler` flushes each answer as it ends (`end` → `drive` → `flush`). So a 16-deep burst becomes 16 task runs, each with its own `sendto`: 44,857 for 43,646 requests.
  - That is about one `sendto` per request against one per 16. The per-turn calls below are a few hundred in all at this load, so they are not the main share here. How much of the rest is the send and how much the per-request task run was not separated.
- **`json`** is not pipelined, so each entry makes one `sendto` per answer (26,759 for 26,741 requests; 25,302 for 24,894), and the send count does not explain its gap.
  - What the async entry adds is a fixed cost per turn of the executor's loop. `nish_async_wait_fd` duplicates the descriptor (`fcntl(F_DUPFD_CLOEXEC)`) and adds it to epoll, `nish_async_fire` removes and closes it, and `serve` writes a wake byte and drains it (the extra `sendto` and the failing `recvfrom`).
  - Here that is about 400 turns: 397 `fcntl`, 399 `close`, 859 `epoll_ctl`. Those are about 2,600 calls on 54,000, or 0.1 a request.
  - So the syscall count does not account for 7–12% either. The rest is CPU per request in user space (the task, its frame, the handler's path), which this run did not profile. The cause of `json`'s gap is open.
- **`db`, `queries`, `updates`, `fortunes`:** `PgAsyncPool` sends one query per connection at a time, where the explicit pool pipelines every request's queries down its 4 connections. A task that finds every connection claimed sleeps 1 ms doubling to 16 ms rather than being woken by a release. The non-2xx answers at 64 and 256 connections are claims that waited out `claimWaitMs`. That is read from the code, not from a trace.
- **`cached-queries`** reads Postgres only to fill the cache, so it is the one database test near the band. The async database entry's peak RSS is lower (12–21 MiB against 65–66 MiB): the explicit entry reserves its carrier's query room up front.

## TechEmpower: where the async entry's time goes, a profile (WP49 stage 6)

[The re-measurement](#techempower-the-async-entry-re-measured-after-the-serve-and-pool-fixes-wp49-stage-6) left `plaintext`, `json` and `db` outside the gate, and their system calls do not explain it. This profile asks where each request's time goes in user space, on the async entry against the explicit one, so that the next fixes have a target. It measures; it fixes nothing. `bash bench/techempower/profile.sh` reproduces it, and `--help` says the method.

Each figure comes from one tool, run in its own phase so that no tool's overhead lands in another's figure. The phases alternate between the two entries.

- **Instructions per request (Ir):** callgrind (valgrind 3.22). The server starts with `--instr-atstart=no`; `callgrind_control -i on` and `-i off` bracket a 10 s wrk window, and Ir per request is the window's program total divided by wrk's responses. Requests in flight at the window's edges are counted on the wrong side, so a figure can be off by at most the in-flight requests over the responses: 4,096 (256 × 16) on `/plaintext`, about 3.5% of the async window; 64 on `/json` and `/db`, under 0.4%. `callgrind_annotate --auto=no` lists the frames, and the script sums a function's lines across the files it was inlined from.
- **Top user-space frames:** `perf record -e cpu-clock:u -F 2999 -g` (perf 6.8.12) over a 5 s wrk window. `cpu-clock:u` is a software event, since the VM exposes no hardware counters. The share is of user-space samples only.
- **System calls per request:** `strace -c -f -p` over a 5 s wrk window.
- **req/s and CPU per request:** wrk and the server's `utime + stime` in `/proc/<pid>/stat`, over the perf window. Both are under perf, so they are not the gate's figures.

All six perf runs were clean (no wrk socket errors, no non-2xx answers). The codec's frames (`http1Next`, `http1TakeLine`, `http1WriteResponseHead`) cost the same on both sides of every test. The difference is in the layer the async entry puts between the codec and the handler, and on `/db` in the pool. The causes below are ranked by the Ir per request each accounts for, from the frames' self Ir (callgrind), with the source line where the count is a line's. The one exception is `/db`'s cause 2, which is kernel time with no Ir figure: it is placed by its system calls (strace) and CPU per request (`/proc`). [WP49 §6](https://github.com/amritk/nish/blob/main/docs/wp49-async-server.md#6-tfb-what-the-top-takes) carries the same ranking as the plan for the fixes.

**`/plaintext` and `/json`** (fix-serve). The same functions make the gap on both. They are ranked by the two tests' Ir summed, and each function's self Ir per request is netted against the explicit side's (plaintext first, then json):

1. **`serveTask` copies each request in and each answer out, byte by byte** (`std/net/serve.ts`), with `HttpRequest.constructor`: 3,158 and 3,428. Of plaintext's: the head copy (`head[i] = src.head[i]` and its loop) about 1,880, the scan for the head's end about 220, the spans about 225, and the new arrays and their zeroing about 170. Then the answer is copied back into the slot (`out[i] = res.body[i]`), about 190, and its content type a character at a time (`type[i] = toU8(res.type.charCodeAt(i))`), about 160.
2. **The `HttpHandler` layer** (`std/net/handler.ts`): `drive`, `start`, `write`, `end`, `enqueue`, `settle` and `flush`, and the `Http1Server`/`Http1Connection` calls they make, 1,313 and 1,596.
3. **`serveOn`'s scans** (`std/net/serve.ts`): 690 and 896, mostly the pass over every slot after each turn (`serveFreeSlot`'s scan about 160, then the `busy`/`done` checks) and `serveType`'s byte compare of the content-type cache, about 70.
4. **The task per request and its memory**: `nish_async_spawn`, `nish_async_done`, `malloc`/`free`/`_int_free` and `nish_arena_release`, 707 and 704.
5. **`Router.match` and `Router.walk`** (`std/net/router.ts`), which the explicit entry does not run: 789 and 562. `routerSameBytes` compares the path a byte at a time.

Against these, the explicit loop's own dispatch (`TfbServer.resume` and `nish_main`, 655 and 919) has no async counterpart. Net of it, the five account for about 6,000 of plaintext's 6,243 and 6,270 of json's 6,539. On `/plaintext` the user-space path is most of a request, so the async entry's CPU per request is 1.7 times the explicit one's. On `/json` the kernel's share of a request (one receive and one send each) is large, so twice the instructions show as 10% more CPU, within the 2.5–17.1% of json's gap in the gate.

**`/db`** (fix-pool; causes 3 and 4 are `serve`'s and go with fix-serve). Causes 1, 3, 4 and 5 are ranked by Ir. Cause 2 is ranked by system calls and CPU time, because its cost is in the kernel:

1. **`pgAsyncRead` zero-fills a fresh 8 KiB buffer per answer** (`std/net/postgres-async.ts`, `new Array<u8>(… PG_ASYNC_INPUT_SIZE)`): `__memset_avx2_unaligned_erms` is 8,360 Ir a request, 22% of the async total, 8,211 of it called from `pgAsyncAnswer`. The answer is then copied twice, a byte at a time: into `carried` in `pgAsyncAnswer` and into that buffer in `pgAsyncRead`, about 1,400. About 9,800 in all.
2. **Two waits per statement, and the system calls they cost.** Each request waits about 2.7 times in `nish_async_wait_fd`: once in `pgAsyncPark` on the pool's wake socket pair for its turn (1.04 a request), once in `pgAsyncTurn` on the Postgres socket (0.99), and `serveOn`'s own wait (0.71). The Postgres socket is also waited on, through `readable(fd)`, in `pgAsyncRead` when a read answers `EAGAIN`. That is where most of the 22 extra system calls a request go: 6.0 `epoll_ctl`, 3.0 `fcntl`, 3.0 `close`, 2.0 `epoll_wait`, 3.0 more `sendto` and 4.9 more `recvfrom`, 1.9 of which answer `EAGAIN`, against the explicit entry's one `sendto` and one `recvfrom`. The Ir is small; the cost is kernel time, most of the 39.7 µs against 9.96. What one wait costs was settled in #1374. What is open here is the number of waits the pool takes per statement.
3. **`serveOn` scans every slot on each pass** (`std/net/serve.ts`), for fix-serve: 5,690 self here against 690 on `/plaintext`, because a request's task stays in flight across its Postgres round trip, so more slots are busy. The `busy && done` check costs about 980, the `busy && hungUp` check about 870, and the loop 640. The `hungUp` call into `HttpHandler.hungUp` and `Http1Server.holds` costs about 1,600. Together, about 4,100.
4. **`serveTask`'s copies**, as on `/plaintext`: 3,988 self.
5. **The seat bookkeeping**: `pgAsyncSkippable` (4 calls a request, each a loop over `PG_ASYNC_MAX_DEPTH`), `pgAsyncSeat`, `pgAsyncSend`, `pgAsyncReady`, `pgAsyncTurn`, `pgAsyncScan`, `pgAsyncSignal` and `pgAsyncPark`, about 3,600 self, against about 1,300 for the explicit carrier's `PgCarrier.next`, `PgCarrier.dispatch` and `TfbDb.turn`. So about 2,300 net.

Net of the explicit carrier's 1,300, the four with an Ir figure (1, 3, 4 and 5) account for about 20,200 of the 26,212.

## TechEmpower: the gate after task recycling (WP49 stage 6)

This re-runs the gate on `main` after two changes.
- **Task recycling (#1493):** a finished task's node keeps its region for the node's next task, so a request's task costs no `malloc` or `free`.
- **Response head (#1457):** a repeated HTTP/1 response head is built once per `Date` tick.

The changes are library and runtime changes that serve every `nish:async` and `serve` program; nothing in `bench/techempower/` moved. Two other candidates were measured and not landed, because what a user would gain does not pay for them:
- **Reading the request in place:** `serveTask` would hand `handle` the connection slot's buffers in place of a copy. The language's task-memory rule refuses this: `handle` may grow what it is given. The copy it would save costs about 330 Ir per request.
- **A fixed-path table in `Router`:** callgrind (valgrind 3.22.0), Ir per match.
  - On `examples/http-app`'s routes: 506 → 491 for `/health`, unchanged for `/notes/17`.
  - On a 44-route router: 591 → 491 and 909 → 765 for fixed paths, unchanged for paths with parameters, and 527 → 570 for a 404.

### A browser-shaped request

1. `http1WriteResponseHead` and everything under it: 1,356 Ir per `/plaintext` request (25.0%) and 1,210 per `/json` (18.3%). Within a Date tick the entry's head is the same bytes every time, so a reused head costs one copy of those bytes instead.

3. `http1PutStatusLine` 77 and `http1ServerReason` 13, which a reused head skips with the rest.

## PostgreSQL client: where the time goes

Not measured by `bench/run.mjs`, which carries this section over unchanged. `node bench/postgres/run.mjs --rounds 12` times the module against libpq ([bench/README.md](https://github.com/amritk/nish/blob/main/bench/README.md#postgresql) describes it); this section records what a profile of the module's side found, and the changes it suggested, none of which paid. 2026-10-27, main at 3a53579, four cores, PostgreSQL and libpq 18.6.

**Reading the tables.** A delta is a variant's median client CPU per query minus main's; + is slower. The noise band is main against its byte-identical copy in the same interleaved rounds, and each table is read against its own band: `list`'s measured 1.35 µs in the baseline and in the decoder table, and 2.06 µs in the buffer table, so 2.06 µs is the larger figure seen.

**The baseline.** `run.mjs --rounds 12`: 12 timed rounds after an untimed warm-up, the binaries interleaved within each round in an order that rotates by one each round. Client CPU per query, median (IQR):

| Workload | Nish | C | Nish − C | Noise band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `pk` | 12.76 µs (0.38) | 15.22 µs (0.71) | −2.47 µs (−16.2 %) | 0.34 µs (2.7 %) |
| `list` | 53.88 µs (4.57) | 67.92 µs (4.95) | −14.03 µs (−20.7 %) | 1.35 µs (2.5 %) |
| `insert` | 32.13 µs (1.73) | 34.71 µs (3.36) | −2.59 µs (−8.1 %) | 0.67 µs (2.1 %) |

All three differences are outside the band. `run.mjs`'s own verdict also asks for a difference larger than both sides' IQRs, and that holds for `pk` and `list` but not for `insert`, whose −2.59 µs is inside C's 3.36 µs IQR, so the tool prints "inside the noise" for it.

**The profile.** `perf record` at 4,999 Hz over 200,000 `pk`, 20,000 `list` and 10,000 `insert` queries, from a speed build whose IR marks the module's decoders and the bench's fold functions `noinline`, so each shows its own self time. In a plain build `PgText.timestamp` is inlined into the bench's `Fold.timestamp` and the other decoders partly into `Bench.run`. Share of all samples, kernel included:

| | `pk` | `list` | `insert` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kernel (socket, scheduler) | 91.2 % | 68.7 % | 92.9 % |
| The module, per query | 2.7 % | 10.5 % | 2.0 % |
| The bench's checksum fold (`Fold.*`, `Bench.*`) | 3.8 % | 17.9 % | 1.7 % |
| libc and the rest | 2.3 % | 2.8 % | 2.7 % |

`insert`'s module figure leaves out `sha256Compress` (1.4 %), SCRAM at connect. On `list` the module's functions are `PgText.timestamp` 2.4 %, `readRow` 2.2 %, `PgArrayWalker.next` 2.1 %, `PgText.int` 1.0 %, `dispatch` 0.9 %, `PgConnection.int` 0.4 %, and no other above 0.25 %. The bench's `Fold.bytes`, a byte-at-a-time checksum like `twin.c`'s, is 12.5 % by itself. On `pk` and `insert` no module function is above 0.7 %. Out of line, every decoder pays a call it does not pay in the shipped build, so 10.5 % of `list` (about 5.7 µs) overstates the module's share.

**Decoding, timed in isolation.** Each decoder called 20 million times on input that changes every call, with a printed checksum, pinned to one core; median of 9 runs, per call:

| | main | changed | the change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `PgText.timestamp`, `2024-01-02 03:04:05.123456+00` | 18.8 ns | 20.6 ns | the 14 digits tested straight-line, an OR of `c - 48` and `57 - c`, in place of counting non-digits over 19 bytes |
| `PgText.int`, 7 digits | 10.3 ns | 9.1 ns | no overflow test per digit when there are at most 18 digits |
| `PgArrayWalker`, a 4-element `text[]` | 65.4 ns | 46.9 ns | an unquoted byte tested against the five refused bytes only when it is under 35, 92 or over 122 |

A `list` query decodes 20 rows of two timestamps, an int and an array, so all of its timestamp decoding is 40 × 18.8 ns, about 0.75 µs; removing it outright could not clear the 1.35 µs band. The walker change, the largest of the three, saves about 0.4 µs a query at 4 elements an array.

**Decoding, timed end to end.** The three changes and all three together, against main and its copy in 12 interleaved rounds (a fixed order within each round, no warm-up round):

| Workload | main | copy (the band) | timestamp | int | walker | all three |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `pk` | 13.54 µs (0.60) | −0.01 | +0.04 | −0.49 | −0.50 | −0.31 |
| `list` | 57.74 µs (7.37) | +1.35 | +2.33 | +1.21 | +0.59 | +1.42 |
| `insert` | 32.84 µs (5.32) | +1.29 | +0.20 | −0.01 | −1.47 | −1.14 |

On `list`, the only workload that decodes timestamps and arrays in bulk, every change measured slower than main, and none was faster by more than the band. The `pk` and `insert` cells outside their bands are noise: neither workload reads an array, yet the walker change moves both by the most. Its 18 ns a call is real, but at about 0.4 µs a `list` query it is about a quarter of the band, so no decoding change ships. Binary results were not tried: integer decoding (`PgText.int` and `PgConnection.int`) is about 1.4 % of `list` and under 0.7 % of the others, and the timestamp and `text[]` columns `list` reads would cost more to decode from binary than from text.

**The input buffer.** `strace -c` shows the module's 8 KiB first input buffer taking a `list` answer in two reads, 5,717 `recvfrom` and as many `epoll_wait` for 3,000 queries against libpq's 3,028. A 16 KiB first size, libpq's own, brings it to 3,028; 32 and 64 KiB to about 3,015. Timed against main in 12 interleaved rounds (a fixed order within each round, no warm-up round):

| Workload | main | main's copy (the band) | 16 KiB | 64 KiB |

| `insert` | 33.10 µs (2.04) | −0.74 (−2.2 %) | +0.86 (2.6 %) | −0.98 (−3.0 %) |

Three cells fall outside their band: `pk` at 64 KiB (+0.57, slower), `insert` at 16 KiB (+0.86, slower) and `insert` at 64 KiB (−0.98, faster). `list` at 16 KiB sits at its band. All are inside main's IQR, and the signs disagree between sizes and workloads. `list`, whose system calls the larger buffer halves, is not faster at either size, so the default stays 8 KiB.

**No system call left to remove.** `strace -c` over 2,000 `list` queries counts 1,990 `sendto` (at depth 16, one per answer that frees a slot), and the 16 KiB buffer above brings `recvfrom` and `epoll_wait` to about one each per query; `insert`, at depth 1, counts 2,007 of each. Each run makes two `epoll_ctl` calls in all.

The module already writes a query's Bind, Execute and Sync as one run of bytes that the caller sends in one call, and every query freed in a wakeup goes in that wakeup's one `sendto`, so fewer writes would need the caller to wait on more queries at once, which is not the module's to choose.

**Growing the buffer on demand, and everything together.** A buffer that starts at 8 KiB and doubles, up to 64 KiB, after a read fills it to its end, so only a connection that receives in bulk pays for the room: `strace` counts 2,023 `recvfrom` for 2,000 `list` queries against main's 3,829.
